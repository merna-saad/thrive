# **COURSE DESCRIPTION** 

Whether you’re asking for a higher salary from your boss, working on a better price for a used car, choosing a movie to watch with your partner, arguing with your roommate over who should do the dishes, or calling your politicians to demand a ceasefire, every day you are negotiating for something. Although negotiations are a ubiquitous part of our professional, personal, and civic lives, many people know little about the strategy and psychology of effective negotiations. Why do we sometimes get our way, while other times we walk away feeling frustrated by our inability to achieve the agreement we desire? How can we better achieve our goals, build and maintain strong relationships with others, and optimize mutual gains? 

Negotiation is the art and science of securing agreements between two or more parties who are seeking to maximize their outcomes and who can ideally improve their outcomes by working together. This course develops your negotiation skills by taking you through a series of simulations and debriefings that reveal the influence of various strategies, tactics, and processes in a wide range of contexts. Each simulation highlights essential concepts that explain performance in negotiation. The course addresses common problems that managers and professionals face during negotiations, and it is designed to complement the technical and diagnostic skills learned in other courses in your curriculum. 

# **COURSE OBJECTIVES** 

First, you should acquire the conceptual skills necessary to become a sophisticated analyst of negotiation situations. Second you should learn a great deal about yourself from repeated exposure to negotiation situations. Third, you should gain an understanding of the psychological processes and biases that underlie the judgment of negotiators. Fourth, you should be able to use this knowledge to secure greater yield in negotiations.  The bottom-line: you should come out of this course as an analytically and intuitively savvy negotiator.

# **FORMAT** 

Students will negotiate in pairs or larger groups at the beginning of class. After that, we will debrief through class discussion of the simulation. The course will build cumulatively from (seemingly) simple negotiations to those of greater complexity.  Structured negotiation exercises are used to isolate and emphasize specific analytic points and essential skills. 

The beginning of the course will focus on **Value Claiming** . These are situations in which each party attempts to enlarge his or her share of what is taken to be a fixed pie.  In this unit, we introduce basic concepts and tactics, with a special emphasis on cognitive biases in negotiation. The middle of the course will focus on **Value Creation** . These sessions will explore negotiations that contain elements of both cooperation and competition; that is, cases where the parties can “enlarge the pie” by creating joint gains.  We identify the potential sources of gains – and why they are often not fully captured, with a special emphasis on the role of perspective taking, communication skills, and trust.  We also analyze the tension between cooperative moves to create value and competitive moves to claim it. This tension is fundamental to negotiation and affects virtually all tactical and strategic choices. The end of the course will focus on **Multiparty Negotiation.** Here, we explore how the dynamics of these negotiations differ from two party settings. In particular, we examine the strategic and tactical issues that are essential to any sophisticated building (or destroying) of coalitions. 

# **NEGOTIATION EXERCISES** 

You will take part in a series of negotiation exercises.  For each case, you will be assigned a particular role, and you will be given background materials, instructions, and (often) confidential information to aid you in your preparation.  You will be paired with one or more negotiating partners.  Typically, you will spend the first part of each class negotiating these cases.  The second part of each class will be spent on debriefing and analysis.  The negotiation exercises are the single most important vehicle for learning in the course. 

Some people may feel uncomfortable negotiating with other class members. Previous students, however, have overwhelmingly found the experience rewarding, particularly the low cost opportunity to “try on” different negotiating approaches. Such experimentation is encouraged as long as it is undertaken seriously. To the extent that your wits and emotions are engaged, the experience promises to help you become a more aware and effective negotiator. 

Our discussion will often focus on how different strategic choices (and interpersonal skills) drive relative success at the bargaining table.  The cases do not involve real money or actual issues, of course, but past students have relished the opportunity to test their effectiveness and to try new techniques they learn in the course.  Because everyone will be negotiating the same case, which will then be extensively discussed, you will get a chance to see something that is seldom visible when you negotiate, i.e., how well you might have done had you negotiated differently.

## **Confidential instructions** 

You will receive confidential instructions in many of the exercises. **Do not show your instructions to others** . You may choose to reveal or discuss some of the contents of your confidential information – indeed this kind of communication is often indispensable “at-the-table.” However, you must not physically show your actual confidential instruction sheets. This rule is intended to mirror reality: in most actual situations, you cannot simply show the full set of your underlying interests and information to your negotiating counterparts. 

The instructions for the exercises are designed to be self-explanatory. Please follow them carefully. Otherwise scoring and comparing scores would become hopelessly complicated. For example, if the instructions of a multi-round exercise say that the negotiators must make their offers simultaneously and in sequence, without discussion, one round at a time, please do so. (It would violate the spirit of the exercise simply to present your counterpart with a signed list of offers for all 20 rounds, as a student once did). The class will function more effectively if we all cooperate in observing these rules. In class discussion, of course, suggestions for more creative resolutions are welcome. For most negotiations, you will need to turn in an outcome report. If you fail to reach an agreement by the deadline, you will be scored accordingly.
