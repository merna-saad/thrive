## **DESCRIPTION** 

Businesses are increasingly using experimentation in a wide range of areas including product pricing, marketing strategies, employee incentives and shaping consumer behavior. These experiments can be particularly effective if the interventions tested incorporate insights from behavioral economics.  However, many companies currently lack the knowledge necessary to take advantage of experimentation. An understanding of how to design and run a good experiment will position students at the cutting edge of decision-making in business. 

The goals of the course are for students to understand: (1) the role of experiments in business (2) how insights from behavioral economics can be incorporated into experimental interventions and (3) how to design and run an experiment in an organization. 

We will begin by discussing the role of experiments in organizations and examining some recent examples. Each student will be responsible for presenting a paper that describes an experiment run in a company or non-profit organization. 

Students will then work individually or in teams to develop an experimental question and design a randomized experiment to test their question of interest. In the design phase, we will discuss methods of experimental design as well as strategies for working with an organization to implement experiments successfully. Students will run pilot studies to inform their designs. At the end of the course, students will present a proposed experimental design and implementation strategy. 

About half of class time will consist of lecture. The other half will be spent workshopping concepts from the lecture and developing students’ proposed experiments. The course is designed to give students the practical tools they need to successfully run experiments in organizations. 

## **OBJECTIVES** 

At the close of this course you will be able to: 

- Understand the role of experimentation in organizations 

- Incorporate insights from behavioral economics into experimental interventions 

- Design and implement randomized experiments 

- Develop experiments that address the interests and concerns of companies and non-profits

## **Supplemental reading:** 

**Module 0: Glennerster and Takavarasha (GT),** **_Running Randomized Evaluations_ , Chapter 2, modules 2.1-2.3 (pp. 24-47).** These modules discusses both experimental and non-experimental methods for measuring _causal impact_ . At the end of the reading, you should understand what causal impact is and be able to discuss the advantages and disadvantages of the different methods. 

**Module 0: Glennerster and Takavarasha (GT),** **_Running Randomized Evaluations_ , Chapter 4, module 4.2 (pp. 108-120).** This module provides a critical overview of randomization. At the end of the reading, you should be able to apply the concepts in the reading to the evaluation and design of experiments; in particular, choosing a level of randomization. 

**Module 6: Glennerster and Takavarasha (GT),** **_Running Randomized Evaluations_ , Chapter 4, module 4.1 (pp. 98-108), 4.4-4.5 (pp. 140161).** These modules discuss opportunities for randomization, randomization designs, and methods for randomizing participants. At the end of the reading, you should be able to apply the concepts in the reading and implement randomizations, including understanding the difference between a lottery design and an encouragement design, as well as the role of stratification (or blocking) in randomizations to achieve balance. 

**Module 7: Reading:** **_Glennerster and Takavarasha (GT) Chapter 6,_ modules 6.1-6.5.** These modules discuss statistical analysis and statistical power. At the end of the reading, you should be able to apply the concepts to choosing appropriate sample sizes for experiments.
